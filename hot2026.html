Syllabus (PDF)

Does market exchange harmonize our interests, or conceal a conflict between them?

We ask this question every week, of every writer we read. Smith, Ricardo, Bentham, Bastiat, Mill, Marx, and the marginalists all give different answers. By December you should be able to say where each of them lands, and why.

Course Description

Economics became a specialized field over several hundred years. Many of its ideas came and went; many were absorbed into what students are taught today. This course covers the period when the foundations were laid: the writers before Adam Smith, then Smith, Malthus, Ricardo, Bentham, Say, Senior, Mill, Bastiat, and Marx, ending with the marginalists in the 1870s.

We stop before 1900. That is deliberate. A course that covers everything from Aristotle to Keynes gives you a list of names. I would rather students read a small number of difficult writers closely.

What You Will Be Able to Do in December

Students arrive believing certain things about markets because they were taught them, and by now those things have come to seem like facts about the world rather than one answer among several. This course makes them strange again by showing the arguments they beat.

Five things. “Understand” is too vague a word for what I want, so each one is something a student will be able to do.

  1. Place an argument in the world that produced it — enclosure, the Industrial Revolution, the Luddites, the move to the cities, the chartered monopolies — and say what about that world made the argument thinkable.
  2. Give an account of a writer's position in his own words and vocabulary, without slipping in ideas he did not have. Where the writers do not fit together, say so rather than forcing them into agreement. They may never fit.
  3. Where a classical argument has a modern counterpart, say what it lines up with and what gets lost in the lining up. Where it has none, say why not.
  4. Account for how economics moved from moral and historical argument to mathematics, and say what that change made easier to see and what it made harder to see.
  5. State a position you disagree with in its strongest form before you argue against it — on the page, and in this room, with each other.

The order of the first three matters. Translating a writer into modern terms before you have read him in his own is how he stops being a person who thought something and becomes a puppet for ideas he never had.

Builds

Six times a semester students build a website that explains one writer's ideas, in the context that produced them. They use an AI agent to build it, and it publishes to a real website anyone can visit.

Why this is in the course. An AI agent can produce cognitive output at almost no cost in time. That is new. For most of history, producing an explanation took hours, and those hours were where the understanding came from. The agent breaks that link. You can have the output without having done the thinking, and you will not necessarily be able to tell the difference. Learning to work with one, and learning what it cannot do for you, is a skill worth having.

How it works. I give the specific assignment before each one, and I show an example of what I am looking for so nobody is guessing. They build it. Then they present it to each other in class.

How it is graded. One thing: did you do it. No rubric, and no partial credit for a prettier page.

You have to be there. The build is not finished when you post it. It is finished when you explain it to someone. Miss presentation day and that build is a zero; if you genuinely cannot be there, send it to me and I will grade it myself.

On AI in This Course

This course requires students to learn to use AI agents.

Most students have used a chatbot. You type a question into ChatGPT and it gives you an answer. An agent is a different thing: you give it a task and it works through multiple steps on its own, writing and running code, until it produces something. Very few undergraduates have used one. I think it is a valuable skill, and there is no better time to learn it than now, so it is part of this course.

That is also why there is a quiz every Tuesday that no AI can help with. Those are not two separate policies. They are one policy.

The Tuesday quiz establishes that a student has done the reading and can do the arithmetic. Once that is established, having an agent build something is reasonable, because they can check its work. Without it, they are approving output they cannot evaluate.

There is also a connection to the material. Smith describes a pin factory where dividing the work raises output enormously, and later in the same book describes what performing one repetitive operation does to the worker who performs it. Marx and Mill both take up that problem. We spend a month on the division of labor, and meanwhile students divide their own work with an agent. That is deliberate and it is worth thinking about.
