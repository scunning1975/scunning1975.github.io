6
What it inspired

Re-estimate with modern DiD. But first: what is the treatment?

SUTVA has two parts. (1) No interference between units. (2) No hidden versions of the treatment: every treated unit gets the same thing.
Their reclassification found part (2) failing. Our “mandatory” states mixed “shall arrest from scratch” with “may arrest, then shall.” Same label, different treatments.
Why it matters. Y(1) − Y(0) is only defined, and only averages coherently across states, if Y(1) means one thing. So must Y(0): every comparison has to be untreated in the same sense.
Where AI agents come in. Tracing every state’s path, defining each treatment, censoring, re-estimating: a huge pain by hand. With their data and an agent, it’s an afternoon.

Three laws: discretionary (D, “may”) · mandatory (M, “shall”) · preferred (P, “should”)

That gives nine treatment effects

Passing a first law
Y(D) − Y(0)a “may arrest” law, from none
Y(M) − Y(0)a “shall arrest” law, from none
Y(P) − Y(0)a “should arrest” law, from none
Tightening
Y(M) − Y(D)may → shall: remove officer discretion
Y(P) − Y(D)may → should: nudge toward arrest
Y(M) − Y(P)should → shall: finish the job
Loosening
Y(D) − Y(M)shall → may: give discretion back
Y(P) − Y(M)shall → should: soften the mandate
Y(D) − Y(P)should → may: drop the preference

All nine are well-defined treatment effects. Which ones can a DiD actually estimate?

The puzzle

DiD needs a comparison group in the same baseline

For states that move from b to a:
{ Y(a)post − Y(b)pre }−{ Y(b)post − Y(b)pre }
movers b → astayers in b
The comparison group has to be in b, before and after. Otherwise its change isn’t Y(b)’s change.
So Y(a) − Y(b) is identified only by states that actually move from b to a. No movers, no DiD for that effect.

Arrows = states that actually make each move, in their data.
